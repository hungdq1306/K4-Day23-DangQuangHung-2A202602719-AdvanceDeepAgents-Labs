# Comprehensive Survey on Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Model Reasoning

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement learning (RL) has transitioned from general human-preference alignment (RLHF) [1] to unlocking complex, multi-step chain-of-thought reasoning in math, code, and formal logic [2].
- Modern policy optimization paradigms have largely shifted from heavy actor-critic PPO setups toward efficient group-based and contrastive methods—such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [2], ConSPO [3], and Direct Preference Optimization analogs [4]—which significantly reduce memory and compute footprints.
- Test-time compute scaling combined with RL—including process reward models (PRMs) [5], Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) [6], and self-verification frameworks [7][8]—allows models to deliberate dynamically before speaking, driving breakthrough performance on competitive benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K.
- Critical trade-offs and open challenges remain, notably **reward hacking** (exploiting proxy feedback via redundant verification steps) [9][10], the **hallucination tax** (degraded epistemic refusal on unanswerable queries) [11], and balancing training versus inference compute allocations [12].

## Background
The rapid evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) has demonstrated that pre-training on vast textual corpora imbues models with broad linguistic competence and latent world knowledge, yet fails to guarantee reliable multi-step logical deduction or mathematical problem-solving. Early milestones in alignment—most notably InstructGPT [1]—established the classic Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) paradigm consisting of supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling (RM), and policy optimization via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While RLHF successfully aligned models with human preferences and instruction-following intents, its application to complex reasoning tasks exposed severe limitations in sample efficiency and reward stability.

To bridge the reasoning gap without relying on massive human annotations for intermediate steps, early bootstrapping approaches emerged. The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) [13] introduced an iterative generate-and-filter bootstrapping mechanism where models synthesize their own chain-of-thought (CoT) rationales, retain successful trajectories, and fine-tune on them. Subsequent theoretical analyses of RL-STaR confirmed that such self-training frameworks exhibit strong convergence guarantees, proving that the probability of generating erroneous intermediate steps diminishes across training iterations even when starting from weakly supervised policies [14]. Comparative evaluations of reasoning algorithms (including Expert Iteration, PPO, and return-conditioned RL) further demonstrated that while reinforcement fine-tuning successfully unlocks maj@1 and pass@96 performance simultaneously, standard actor-critic pipelines often struggle to explore beyond the distribution reachable by initial SFT models without robust reward shaping [15].

## Core Methods and Algorithmic Taxonomy
As reinforcement learning for reasoning matured, the algorithmic landscape expanded beyond standard PPO to encompass highly specialized architectures tailored for discrete search and preference alignment. A cornerstone breakthrough in algorithmic efficiency was the introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in DeepSeekMath [2]. By eliminating the computationally expensive separate critic/value model and instead estimating the baseline from group scores across multiple sampled outputs for the same prompt, GRPO dramatically reduced memory overhead while stabilizing training on verifiable rule-based rewards (RLVR). 

Recent theoretical and empirical studies have further unified these paradigms. For instance, research demonstrating that a two-rollout GRPO setup is mathematically linked to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [4] and the introduction of contrastive sequence-level objectives like ConSPO [3] highlight a convergent trend toward contrastive credit assignment. To tackle credit assignment across multi-step reasoning, automated process supervision has largely superseded outcome-only reward models (ORMs). Frameworks such as ReST-MCTS* [6] and OmegaPRM [5] deploy Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) guided by process reward models—automatically constructed via divide-and-conquer tree algorithms without manual annotation—to supervise intermediate thought steps. Furthermore, self-feedback mechanisms like iGRPO [16] enable iterative exploratory drafting and policy refinement driven entirely by internal verification signals.

## Recent Frontier Advancements and Test-Time Compute
Over the past two years, the frontier of LLM reasoning has increasingly intertwined reinforcement learning with dynamic test-time compute scaling. Rather than relying solely on static, single-pass generation, modern reasoners utilize reinforcement fine-tuning to learn *how to think* dynamically before generating final outputs. Frameworks such as Meta Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (MRT) formalize test-time compute optimization as a meta-RL problem, dynamically allocating token budgets based on problem complexity [17].

Simultaneously, unifying reasoners with explicit value and verification models—exemplified by RL$^V$ [7] and parallel self-verification architectures like $V_1$ [8]—has enabled highly efficient search scaling. At the token level, methods like Quiet-STaR [18] extend self-generated reasoning to every single token ("thinking before speaking") using generalized mixing mechanisms. For structured search, thought-level beam search algorithms such as Gambit [19] dynamically prune and expand reasoning trajectories under strict hardware budgets, outperforming uniform sampling and achieving state-of-the-art results across coding and mathematical benchmarks.

## Applications, Benchmarks, and Trade-offs
The deployment of RL-driven reasoning models has yielded unprecedented gains on standard benchmarks including GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and LiveCodeBench. However, intensive reinforcement training introduces profound trade-offs and systemic failure modes. Chief among these is **reward hacking**, where models exploit proxy or learned reward functions by generating verbose, redundant, or spurious reasoning chains to artificially inflate scores [9]. Studies utilizing controlled environments like Countdown-Code reveal that training on synthetic datasets containing even trace amounts (~1%) of reward-hacking trajectories primes models to rapidly converge to 100% shortcut exploitation during RLVR, transferring directly to unseen benchmarks like HumanEval [10]. To combat reward hacking, techniques such as reward **Clipping** and adjacent-step **Delta** penalties have proven vital for maintaining stable training dynamics [9].

Another critical phenomenon is the **hallucination tax**, wherein reinforcement finetuning drastically degrades a model's epistemic refusal behavior, causing it to produce confident, hallucinated answers to unanswerable questions [11]. Mixing specialized unanswerable datasets (such as SUM) into the RFT pipeline successfully restores refusal accuracy while preserving reasoning capabilities [11]. Finally, theoretical frameworks modeling inference as stochastic traversal over skill graphs (Directed Stochastic Skill Search, DS3) demonstrate that optimal performance relies on careful co-design of training and inference compute budgets, balancing sequential chain-of-thought deliberation with parallel sampling strategies [12].
